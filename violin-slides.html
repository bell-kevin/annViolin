The Origin of the
Modern Violin

Ann Peterson A fifteen-minute talk

To begin with

Every other instrument has a childhood.

The piano — gradual, documented, improved

Clavichord→ Harpsichord→ Fortepiano→ Concert grand

The violin — abrupt, and essentially finished

?→ c. 1530 · the violin

Four strings tuned in fifths. An arched top and back carved from solid wood. Two f-holes. A scroll. That shape has not meaningfully changed in five hundred years.

Before anything else

The real invention is not the box.
It is the bow.

Plucked strings are ancient and universal. Drawing a ribbon of horsehair across a string, so the sound never has to stop, is a specific idea — and it came from somewhere else.

  • 9th–10th c.The bowed string emerges among the horse cultures of Central Asia.
  • Two roadsWest through the Byzantine empire, and west through Islamic Spain.
  • c. 1000Bowed instruments are established in Europe. Everything after this is five centuries of working out what to put underneath the bow.

The inheritance

Three ancestors, three bequests

from the Arabic rabāb

Rebec

Small, narrow, pear-shaped, carved from one block. Three strings. Loud, nasal, and made for dancing.

gave the tuning in fifths
13th–15th century

Medieval fiddle

Bigger and flatter. A waisted soundbox, often five strings, with a drone running off the side of the fingerboard.

gave the body
Renaissance Italy

Lira da braccio

Seven strings, a bridge, sound holes, a waist — and painters put it in the hands of Apollo and Orpheus.

gave the playing position

The violin is not the descendant of any one of them. It is a synthesis of all three.

Clearing away a misunderstanding

The viol is a cousin, not a parent

Northern Italy · the Po valley · 1520s–1550s

There is no birth certificate.
There are three kinds of evidence.

1520s–1530s

The word

Variants of violino begin appearing in account books and court records — payments to players of an instrument that had not needed a name before.

1529 & 1535

The pictures

Gaudenzio Ferrari paints instruments we can identify on sight — and within six years he is painting the whole family.

1556

The description

Philibert Jambe de Fer sets out the family in print, tuning and all, and tells us plainly who played it.

All three converge on the same twenty years.

Gaudenzio Ferrari · Piedmont and Lombardy

The first pictures of a violin

  • c. 1529Vercelli. In the Madonna of the Orange Tree, an angel holds what is unmistakably a violin — three strings rather than four, but the body, the f-holes, the scroll, the whole gesture of it, all there.
  • 1535Saronno. Ferrari paints the dome of the sanctuary: a ceiling crowded with musical angels — and among them not one violin but the whole family. Violin, viola, and a big bass.

Six years after the first glimpse of the idea, a painter depicts it as a complete, settled, four-part consort. This thing developed fast.

Lyon · 1556

The viol is what gentlemen and merchants play to pass the time. The violin is used for dancing — and it is played by those who make a living from it.

Philibert Jambe de Fer, Epitome musical, paraphrased —
the first printed description of the violin family, and of its tuning: G · D · A · E

The violin was not born noble. It was born a working instrument — for weddings, dances, processions and the theatre. It took most of a century to be let into the drawing room.

Cremona · c. 1505–1577

Andrea Amati did not invent the violin.
He resolved it.

He is as close to “the inventor” as any one person gets — and even that is not quite right. What he settled was everything that makes the design repeatable.

  • OutsideThe proportions, the arching, the shape of the f-hole.
  • InsideCorner blocks and linings — the architecture that lets a thin wooden box survive fifty pounds of string tension for four hundred years.
  • AfterHe taught it to his sons.

1564 · the oldest violins on earth

38

instruments supplied by Andrea Amati to the court of Charles IX of France — painted with the royal arms and mottoes in gold.

One generation after those angels at Saronno, the King of France ordered his entire court string band from a workshop in Cremona.

A handful survive — one at the Ashmolean in Oxford, several at the National Music Museum in Vermillion, South Dakota. These are the oldest violins that still exist anywhere in the world.

Forty miles away

Cremona was not alone

  • BresciaZanetto and Peregrino Micheli, then Gasparo da Salò (1540–1609), then Giovanni Paolo Maggini.
  • The styleBolder. Less refined. Sometimes frankly strange — and wonderful. Brescian double basses are prized to this day.
and yet

The Brescian line dies out in the seventeenth century. The Cremonese line does not.

Which raises the question the rest of this talk turns on: why Cremona?

Four answers, none of them magic

Why Cremona?

Wood

Slow-grown alpine spruce for the tops, down from the Val di Fiemme in the Dolomites — still cut for instruments today. Maple for the backs, up the trade routes from the Balkans.

Position

Cremona sits on the Po. Materials in, instruments out.

Money

Northern Italian courts and churches with the means to commission — and the appetite.

Workshop culture

The one people forget. Cremona had no restrictive guild governing violin making. The skill passed by apprenticeship, in the house, hand to hand, for two hundred years.

Not one genius — an unbroken chain

Two temperaments

c. 1644–1737

Antonio Stradivari

Lives to about ninety-three and works nearly to the end. Perhaps 1,100 instruments made; around 650 survive.

Between 1700 and 1720 — the golden period — he settles on a longer, flatter, broader pattern under a deep red-orange varnish, and makes the instruments against which every violin since has been measured.

1698–1744

Giuseppe Guarneri “del Gesù”

A generation later, and the opposite temperament. Faster, rougher, more irregular, more daring.

Paganini’s instrument — Il Cannone — and the one a great many soloists still reach for when they want sheer power.

The question everyone asks

So what was the secret?

Proposed, and none of it settled

  • VarnishA lost recipe or a lost ground layer.
  • ChemistrySome mineral or fungal treatment of the wood.
  • ClimateThe Little Ice Age producing unusually dense, slow-grown spruce in exactly those decades.
Since 2010 · blind playing tests

Soloists could not reliably tell old from new.

In a darkened room, wearing welder’s goggles, players compared old Cremonese instruments against fine modern ones. Asked to pick a favourite, they often picked the new one.

Which does not diminish Stradivari. The real secret is less romantic and rather more impressive: two hundred years of concentrated craft in one small city, refining a single design, passed hand to hand without a break.

The twist · c. 1780–1830 · Paris and Mirecourt

Almost no Stradivari is as Stradivari left it

Paris · c. 1785

The bow was rebuilt too

  • The old bowSuperb at articulating, at dancing, at making each note speak.
  • The new bowFrançois Tourte, working alongside the violinist Viotti, arrives at a bow that can sing one long line. Romantic violin playing is simply not possible without it.
  • c. 1820Louis Spohr adds the chin rest. The shoulder rest is a twentieth-century afterthought.

  • Assembledaround 1530 in northern Italy, out of a rebec’s tuning, a fiddle’s body, and a lira da braccio’s playing position.
  • Perfectedin Cremona, within a single generation.
  • Rebuilttwo hundred and fifty years later — quietly, and to be louder.

The instrument in the case is a Renaissance object wearing a Romantic conversion.

And it is still, five centuries on, the best answer anyone has found to the question: what should a singing voice made of wood look like?
