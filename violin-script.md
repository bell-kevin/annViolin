## [SLIDE 1 — Title] · 0:00

Good afternoon, and thank you for having me.

I want to start with a puzzle.

## [SLIDE 2 — A puzzle] · 0:15

Almost every instrument we play has a long, visible childhood. You can watch the piano grow up — clavichord, harpsichord, fortepiano, concert grand. Each step is documented. Each step is a little better than the one before. The flute, the clarinet, the horn — same story. Awkward ancestors, gradual improvement.

The violin does not do that.

The violin appears in northern Italy in the 1530s, and it appears essentially **finished**. Within about thirty years of the first pictures of it, one workshop in Cremona is building instruments we would recognize instantly, that we would happily play tonight, and that have never really been improved on. Four strings tuned in fifths. An arched top and back carved from solid wood. Two f-holes. A scroll.

That shape has not meaningfully changed in five hundred years.

So — where did it come from? And why did it arrive so complete?

## [SLIDE 3 — The bow came first] · 1:10

Let's start further back than you might expect, because the real invention here is not the box. It's the **bow**.

Plucked strings are ancient and universal. But drawing a ribbon of horsehair across a string to make a sound that never has to stop — that is a specific idea, and it seems to have come from the horse cultures of Central Asia, somewhere around the ninth or tenth century. It traveled west by two roads: through the Byzantine empire, and through Islamic Spain. By about the year 1000, Europe has bowed instruments.

Everything that follows is Europe spending five hundred years working out what to put underneath that bow.

## [SLIDE 4 — Three ancestors] · 1:55

By the end of the Middle Ages, three instruments matter to our story.

The **rebec**. Small, narrow, pear-shaped, carved from a single block of wood. Usually three strings — and this is the important part — **tuned in fifths**. It descends from the Arabic rabāb. It was loud, it was nasal, and it was a dance instrument.

The **medieval fiddle**, or vielle. Bigger. Flatter. Waisted, with a real soundbox, often five strings, and a drone string running off the side of the fingerboard. This is the instrument of the court and of the storyteller.

And the **lira da braccio** — the Renaissance Italian instrument, and the one that gets closest. Seven strings: five over the fingerboard, two drones off the edge. A waisted body. A bridge. Sound holes. And crucially, it was played *da braccio* — **on the arm**. Painters put it in the hands of Apollo and of Orpheus. It belonged to the improvising poet-musician.

Look at a lira da braccio and you are essentially looking at a viola.

So the violin inherits its **tuning** from the rebec, its **body** from the fiddle, and its **playing position** from the lira da braccio. It is not a descendant of any one of them. It is a synthesis.

## [SLIDE 5 — Not the viol] · 3:15

Now let me clear away the most common misunderstanding, which is that the violin came from the viol — the viola da gamba.

It didn't. They are **cousins, not parent and child**. They were developed at the same time, in the same places, often by the same hands, for completely different purposes.

The viol has six strings, tuned in fourths with a third in the middle, like a lute. It has **frets** — actual gut frets tied around the neck. Sloped shoulders. A flat back. C-shaped sound holes. You hold it on or between the legs — *da gamba*, on the leg — and you hold the bow **underhand**.

The violin has four strings tuned in fifths. **No frets.** Square shoulders with pointed corners. A back carved and arched from solid wood. F-holes. You hold it on the arm, and the bow overhand.

Those are two different design philosophies, and you can hear the difference. The viol is built for **polyphony** — for chords, for playing one line among six in a quiet room. The violin is built to be a **single loud singing voice**, heard across a hall, or across a piazza, over the noise of dancing feet.

## [SLIDE 6 — Northern Italy, 1520s–1550s] · 4:30

Which brings us to the actual moment of invention: northern Italy, the Po valley, the second quarter of the sixteenth century.

There is no birth certificate. What we have is three different kinds of evidence, and they converge on the same twenty years.

First, the **word**. Variants of *violino* start turning up in account books and court records in the 1520s and 1530s — payments to players of an instrument that had not needed a name before.

## [SLIDE 7 — The first pictures] · 5:15

Second, the **pictures** — and these are wonderful.

The full painting on the left is **Gaudenzio Ferrari's** *Madonna of the Orange Tree*, painted around **1529** in **Vercelli**. Now look at the enlarged detail in the center. The angel on the left holds a violin. Three strings rather than four — but the curved waist, the f-holes, the scroll: **unmistakable**. These are the features we still recognize on a violin today.

*(Point to the violin in the enlarged detail. Pause a beat.)*

Then in **1535** Ferrari paints the dome of the sanctuary at Saronno: a ceiling crowded with musical angels. Among them is the **whole family** — violin, viola, and a big bass.

Six years after this glimpse in Vercelli, a painter is depicting a complete, settled, four-part consort. **This thing developed fast.**

## [SLIDE 8 — "Used for dancing"] · 6:05

Third, the **description**. In **1556**, a Frenchman named Philibert Jambe de Fer publishes a small music primer in Lyon, and he lays out the violin family properly — including the tuning: **G, D, A, E**. Exactly what is on your instrument tonight.

But he also tells us something about status, and this is my favorite sentence in the whole story. He says, in effect: the viol is the instrument that gentlemen and merchants play to pass their time. The violin is **used for dancing**, and it is played by those who make a living from it.

The violin was not born noble. It was born a **working instrument** — a professional's tool for weddings, dances, processions, and the theater. It took the better part of a century to be let into the drawing room.

## [SLIDE 9 — Andrea Amati] · 7:00

So who actually built it?

The form is fixed — standardized, made repeatable — in Cremona, in the workshop of **Andrea Amati**, born around 1505, died 1577. He is as close to "the inventor of the violin" as any one person gets, and even that is not quite right.

He did not invent it out of nothing. What he did was **resolve** it. He settled the proportions. The arching. The shape of the f-hole. The corner blocks, the linings, the whole internal architecture that lets a thin wooden box survive fifty pounds of string tension for four hundred years.

And then — this is the part that mattered — he taught it to his sons, so that it could be **repeated**.

## [SLIDE 10 — A commission from a king] · 7:50

The oldest violins that still exist anywhere in the world are his.

In the 1560s, Amati supplies a large set of instruments — the traditional figure is **thirty-eight** — to the court of **Charles IX of France**, decorated with the royal arms and mottoes in gold. A handful survive. There is one in Oxford, and several in South Dakota.

Consider what that commission means. In 1564, the King of France orders his entire court string band from a workshop in Cremona. **One generation** after those angels at Saronno, this brand-new instrument is already the establishment choice — and Cremona is already the address.

## [SLIDE 11 — Brescia] · 8:40 · [CUT IF LONG]

Cremona was not alone. Forty miles away, **Brescia** had a rival school — Zanetto and Peregrino Micheli, then **Gasparo da Salò**, then Giovanni Paolo Maggini.

Brescian instruments are bolder, less refined, sometimes frankly strange — and wonderful. Their double basses are prized to this day. But the Brescian line dies out in the seventeenth century. The Cremonese line does not, and the reason is instructive.

## [SLIDE 12 — Why Cremona?] · 9:15

Why Cremona? Several answers, none of them magic.

**Wood.** Spruce for the tops came down from the Val di Fiemme in the Dolomites — slow-grown alpine spruce, still cut for instruments today. Maple for the backs came up the trade routes from the Balkans.

**Position.** Cremona sits on the Po. Materials in, instruments out.

**Money.** Northern Italian courts and churches with the means to commission.

And the one people forget: **workshop culture**. Cremona had no restrictive guild governing violin making. The skill passed by apprenticeship, in the house, hand to hand, for two hundred years.

## [SLIDE 13 — The chain] · 10:10

And that is the real story of Cremona. Not one genius. An **unbroken chain**.

Andrea Amati passes it to his sons Antonio and Girolamo. Girolamo's son is **Nicolò Amati**, born 1596 — the finest maker of the family.

And then something terrible turns out to be decisive.

In **1630**, plague comes through Cremona. It kills a huge share of the city, including most of the working luthiers — and including Nicolò's own father, mother, and sisters. Nicolò survives. And for the first time, the family has to take apprentices from **outside** the family, because there is no one left inside it.

One of those apprentices is **Andrea Guarneri**. And somewhere in Nicolò's orbit — the exact relationship is still argued over by scholars — is a young man named **Antonio Stradivari**.

The plague is what opened the door.

## [SLIDE 14 — Stradivari and del Gesù] · 11:10

Stradivari lives to about ninety-three and works nearly to the end. He makes perhaps **eleven hundred** instruments; around **six hundred and fifty** survive.

Between roughly **1700 and 1720** — what dealers call his golden period — he settles on a slightly longer, flatter, broader pattern under a deep red-orange varnish, and produces the instruments against which every violin since has been measured.

Then, a generation later, **Giuseppe Guarneri, called "del Gesù."** Faster, rougher, more daring, more irregular. Paganini's instrument. And the one a great many soloists reach for when they want sheer power.

## [SLIDE 15 — So what was the secret?] · 12:00

Now — the question I would be asked in a moment if I did not get to it first. **What was the secret?**

A great many have been proposed. The varnish. Some chemical treatment of the wood. The theory that the Little Ice Age produced unusually dense, slow-grown spruce in exactly those decades. Each of these has some evidence behind it. **None of them is settled.**

But here is the honest finding. Since 2010, researchers have run properly **blind** tests — soloists in a darkened room wearing welder's goggles, playing old Cremonese instruments against fine modern ones. The players could not reliably tell which was which. And asked to pick a favorite, they often picked the **new** one.

I don't offer that to diminish Stradivari. I offer it because I think the real secret is less romantic and rather more impressive: **two hundred years of concentrated craft in one small city**, refining a single design, passed hand to hand without a break.

## [SLIDE 16 — The twist] · 13:00

And now I owe you the word in my title, because I said the ***modern*** violin.

Here is the twist. **Almost no Stradivari played today is in the condition Stradivari left it.**

Between roughly **1780 and 1830**, essentially every surviving old Italian violin was **rebuilt**. Concert halls got bigger. Orchestras got bigger. Pitch went up. So makers — largely in Paris and Mirecourt — took these instruments apart and modernized them.

A **longer neck**, tilted back at an angle, and **mortised into the body** instead of nailed to it. A **longer ebony fingerboard**, to reach the high positions the new music demanded. A **higher bridge**. And inside, a **longer, heavier bass bar** to carry the extra tension.

More tension. More projection. More brilliance.

## [SLIDE 17 — And the bow] · 13:55

The bow was rebuilt too.

Around **1785**, **François Tourte** in Paris — working alongside the violinist Viotti — arrives at the modern bow. Pernambuco wood. Longer, heavier, and curved **inward** instead of outward, with a frog that spreads the hair into a flat ribbon.

The baroque bow is superb at articulating, at dancing, at speaking each note. The Tourte bow can **sing one long line**. Romantic violin playing is simply not possible without it.

Louis Spohr adds the chin rest around 1820. The shoulder rest is a twentieth-century afterthought.

So — the whole arc on a single line.

## [SLIDE 19 — Closing] · 14:55

Here is where we land.

The violin was **assembled** around 1530 in northern Italy, out of a rebec's tuning, a fiddle's body, and a lira da braccio's playing position. It was **perfected** in Cremona within a single generation. And then, two hundred and fifty years later, it was quietly **rebuilt** to be louder.

The instrument in the case is a Renaissance object wearing a Romantic conversion.

And it is still, five centuries on, the best answer anyone has found to the question: *what should a singing voice made of wood look like?*

Thank you.

## If someone asks…

**"Is my grandmother's violin with the Stradivarius label real?"**
Almost certainly not — and it is usually not a fake in the dishonest sense. From the 1880s onward, German and French factories turned out hundreds of thousands of student violins with "Antonius Stradivarius Cremonensis" printed inside as a statement of *model*, not of origin — rather the way a car might be sold as a "roadster." It is still worth having a good shop look at it, but the label by itself means nothing.

**"How many real Stradivari are there?"**
Around 650 surviving instruments out of roughly 1,100 made — about 500 of them violins, plus violas, cellos, and a few guitars and harps.

**"Why f-holes?"**
Two reasons. Acoustically, a long thin slot moves far more air at the frequencies that matter than a round hole of the same area. Structurally, the shape leaves a bar of wood on either side to carry the bridge load. The medieval C-hole evolved into it gradually.

**"Did women play the violin then?"**
Not publicly, and not for a long time — it was a professional's instrument, and the professions were closed to them. The great exception is Venice: the *ospedali*, the charitable foundling hospitals, trained orphaned girls into celebrated orchestras. Vivaldi wrote much of his violin music for the young women of the Pietà.

**"What did it sound like then?"**
Lower and softer. Baroque pitch varied by city but often sat near A=415, roughly a semitone below ours. Plain gut strings, a lighter bass bar, and a shorter bow — sweeter and more speech-like, with far less carrying power.

## Sources and further reading

- David Schoenbaum, *The Violin: A Social History of the World's Most Versatile Instrument* (2013) — the best single-volume general history.
- Peter Walls, "Violin," *Grove Music Online* — the standard scholarly summary of the origins question.
- Philibert Jambe de Fer, *Epitome musical* (Lyon, 1556) — the first description of the violin family and its tuning.
- Claudia Fritz et al., "Player preferences among new and old violins," *PNAS* (2012), and the Paris follow-up study (2014) — the blind playing tests.
- The Ashmolean Museum, Oxford — holds "Le Messie" (Stradivari, 1716) and an Andrea Amati from the Charles IX set.
- National Music Museum, Vermillion, South Dakota — the largest American collection of early Cremonese instruments, including Amatis from the French court set.
